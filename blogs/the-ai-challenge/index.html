The AI Challenge

Fritz Antonette Ardael | Jun 20, 2026 min read

Innovation Is Moving Faster Than Regulation

“Artificial intelligence isn’t waiting for society to catch up. The question is—are we ready?”

Have you ever noticed that every time you open social media, there seems to be another AI tool everyone is talking about? One week, AI is creating amazing artwork. The next, it is generating realistic videos, writing code, helping students study, or assisting doctors in detecting diseases.

It is exciting, but honestly, it can also be hard to keep up.

As someone currently studying data science, I find the rapid growth of AI both fascinating and a little overwhelming. Every time I feel like I am finally catching up with the latest AI trend, something even newer appears.

Before taking this course, I mostly focused on what AI could do. Now, I find myself asking a different question:

How do we make sure AI develops responsibly?

That question stayed with me throughout this course because AI is not slowing down anytime soon. The real challenge is no longer simply building smarter AI systems. It is making sure that our laws, ethical frameworks, organizations, and society can keep up with them.

AI Is Moving Faster Than Ever

Sometimes AI reminds me of that one friend who somehow learns a new hobby every weekend. One week, they are baking bread. The next, they are learning photography. Before you know it, they are running marathons while you are still trying to understand what happened. AI feels exactly like that.

Every week seems to bring another breakthrough, and it is incredible to see how quickly AI is changing different industries. AI is already being used to:

  • Assist doctors in detecting diseases earlier
  • Help businesses analyze data and make faster decisions
  • Support students through personalized learning tools
  • Automate repetitive tasks so people can focus on more meaningful work
  • Help researchers discover patterns hidden in massive datasets

These advancements are exciting because they demonstrate how AI can improve people’s lives. However, every powerful technology comes with responsibility.

The more capable AI becomes, the more important it is to ask not only what it can do, but also what it should do.

AI Is Powerful—but Human Choices Shape Its Impact

One of the biggest lessons I learned during this course is that technology does not exist separately from human choices.

A hammer can build a house or break a window. The internet allows us to communicate with people around the world, but it can also be used for scams, cybercrime, and misinformation. AI is similar, although its risks can be more complicated.

Some AI-related harms are caused by deliberate misuse. Others may result from biased data, poor system design, inadequate testing, a lack of transparency, or organizations deploying systems without fully understanding their consequences.

That is why, instead of asking only: “Can we build this?”

I think developers, organizations, and policymakers should also ask:

  • Should we build it?
  • Who benefits from it?
  • Who could be harmed?
  • Could someone misuse it?
  • Is it fair to everyone?
  • Can people understand or challenge its decisions? Who should be accountable when something goes wrong?

Before taking this course, I probably would not have thought deeply about these questions. Now, I believe they are just as important for a data scientist as learning Python, statistics, or machine learning.

When Innovation Creates New Risks

OWhenever a new technology appears, people find creative ways to use it. Most people use technology to solve problems, improve productivity, and make life easier.

Unfortunately, others find ways to use the same technology to deceive, manipulate, and cause harm.

AI has already raised serious concerns about its potential use in:

  • Creating convincing phishing messages
  • Cloning voices to impersonate real people
  • Producing realistic deepfake videos and audio
  • Spreading misinformation and fabricated content
  • Making scams more convincing and scalable
  • Assisting or automating certain cyberattacks

One controversy that caught my attention involved a famous South Korean actor who became the subject of serious allegations in March 2025. The controversy spread rapidly through YouTube and social media and had major consequences for his career, endorsements, and entertainment projects.

The case became particularly relevant to my interest in AI governance because some materials connected to the wider controversy were disputed, while questions were raised publicly about the authenticity and possible manipulation of digital evidence. The actor denied key allegations, and legal complaints and investigations followed.

It is important to be careful when discussing an ongoing and disputed case. However, the broader issue it highlights is worth considering: What happens when realistic fake or manipulated digital evidence can spread faster than experts, investigators, journalists, or courts can verify it? In the age of generative AI, seeing or hearing something is no longer always enough to prove that it is real.

By the time the truth is established, the damage may already be difficult to reverse. A person’s reputation can be damaged within days. Contracts can disappear, projects can be cancelled, and years of work can be overshadowed by a viral accusation. Even when false or misleading information is eventually corrected, the original content may already have reached millions of people.

This problem extends far beyond celebrities. Ordinary people can also become targets of impersonation, harassment, scams, misinformation, and manipulated media.

South Korea’s AI Basic Act took effect in January 2026, establishing a national framework focused on AI development, transparency, safety, and trust. The timing highlights a broader challenge facing governments around the world: legal safeguards and governance frameworks can take years to develop, while the risks associated with new technologies can emerge much faster.

Cases like these made me realize that AI-related harms are not simply problems that we might face someday in the future.

They are challenges society is already dealing with today.

Why Regulation Struggles to Keep Up

One thing that surprised me during this course is how slowly laws can change compared with technology.

This does not necessarily mean governments do not care. Creating legislation takes time. New laws may require research, expert advice, public consultation, political debate, amendments, and approval before they can take effect.

Meanwhile, AI continues to evolve.

By the time lawmakers begin discussing one problem, a new capability or risk may already have appeared.

Today, governments are facing questions such as:

  • Who owns AI-generated artwork?
  • Can someone’s voice legally be cloned?
  • Should AI-generated videos always be labelled?
  • Who is responsible when an AI system causes harm?
  • How much personal data should AI systems be allowed to collect?
  • What rights should people have when AI makes decisions about them?

These are not easy questions.

The challenge is finding a balance between encouraging innovation and protecting people from harm. If regulation is too weak, individuals and communities may be exposed to unnecessary risks. If regulation is poorly designed or overly rigid, it could limit beneficial innovation.

This is why I believe AI regulation must be adaptable. Governments cannot predict every future AI development, but they can create principles, institutions, and risk-management systems that are flexible enough to respond when technology changes.

Canada’s AIDA and the EU AI Act

The differences between regulatory approaches around the world show how difficult AI governance can be.

Canada attempted to introduce legislation specifically addressing artificial intelligence through the proposed Artificial Intelligence and Data Act (AIDA). AIDA was introduced as part of Bill C-27 and aimed to establish requirements for certain high-impact AI systems, including responsibilities related to risk, harm, bias, transparency, and accountability.

However, AIDA did not become law because Bill C-27 did not complete the legislative process before Parliament was prorogued in January 2025. As a result, Canada remains without a comprehensive federal law specifically regulating AI in the private sector.

The European Union has taken a different approach through the EU AI Act, which entered into force in August 2024 and is being implemented gradually.

The EU AI Act follows a risk-based approach. In simple terms, the greater the potential risk of an AI system, the stronger the legal requirements.

Certain AI practices are prohibited, while high-risk systems are subject to stricter obligations related to areas such as risk management, data governance, documentation, transparency, human oversight, and monitoring.

The difference between these approaches is interesting. Canada recognized the need for AI-specific regulation, but its proposed framework did not complete the legislative process. The European Union, meanwhile, has moved from proposal to implementation.

For me, this raises an important question: How long can countries rely mainly on existing laws and voluntary ethical guidelines while AI technology continues to evolve so quickly?

Laws Alone Aren’t Enough

Before taking this course, I assumed governments could simply create enough laws to solve AI’s ethical challenges.

Now I realize it is not that simple.

Laws are necessary, but laws usually establish the minimum standard of acceptable behaviour. Ethical responsibility often requires organizations and developers to go further.

This is why I found the concept of soft ethics so interesting.

Soft ethics encourages organizations and developers to move beyond simply asking: “Is this legal?”

It also asks: “Is this the right thing to do?”

A company might technically be allowed to use certain data, for example, but should it use that data if users do not fully understand how it will be used? An AI system might be legally deployable, but should an organization use it if the system has not been adequately tested for bias? To me, this is what Responsible AI is really about.

It means creating and using AI systems that are not only intelligent and technically capable, but also fair, transparent, accountable, safe, and respectful of people’s rights—even before regulations require organizations to do so.

Could AI Help Governments Too?

While learning about AI governance, I started wondering whether governments could use AI as part of the solution.

I am not suggesting that AI should replace lawmakers, policymakers, investigators, or judges. Human judgment and accountability should remain central to important decisions. However, AI can analyze large amounts of information and identify patterns that humans might overlook.

Imagine AI tools helping governments:

  • Detect emerging cybercrime trends
  • Identify patterns in new online scams
  • Monitor emerging technological risks
  • Simulate possible ways future technologies could be misused
  • Analyze large volumes of public consultation data
  • Support policymakers with data-driven insights

Of course, governments using AI would create ethical questions of its own. Public-sector AI systems would still need transparency, accountability, privacy protection, security, human oversight, and appropriate limits.

Still, I find the idea fascinating.

The same technology creating new challenges for governments could also become one of the tools used to understand and respond to those challenges.

Building AI People Can Trust

Another topic that changed my perspective was AI governance.

Before this course, I thought governance simply meant creating more rules. Now, I understand it more broadly. Governance is about establishing responsibilities, processes, oversight, and accountability throughout the life cycle of an AI system.

Most importantly, good AI governance is about building trust.

Responsible AI can be guided by ethical principles such as:

  • Beneficence – AI should contribute to human and social well-being.
  • Non-maleficence – AI should avoid causing unnecessary harm.
  • Justice – AI systems should promote fairness and avoid discrimination.
  • Autonomy – People should maintain meaningful control over important decisions affecting their lives.
  • Explicability – AI systems and the decisions surrounding their use should be transparent and understandable.

At first, these principles sounded like abstract philosophical ideas to me. Now, they feel practical.

They influence real questions about how data is collected, how models are trained, how systems are tested, how decisions are explained, and who becomes responsible when something goes wrong.

These are questions that every future data scientist should learn to ask.

Where Do We Go From Here?

Based on these challenges, I believe responsible AI governance requires action at several levels.

Governments need adaptable regulations that can respond to emerging technologies without unnecessarily preventing beneficial innovation.

Organizations need strong internal AI governance. Ethical responsibility should not begin only when a law forces a company to act.

Developers and data scientists need to think beyond technical performance. A model with high accuracy is not automatically a good system if it is unfair, unsafe, invasive, or impossible to challenge.

Technology companies and online platforms need effective ways to respond to AI-generated impersonation, manipulated media, scams, and harmful misinformation.

Everyday users also have responsibilities. We should think critically about what we share, verify information before spreading it, and use AI tools responsibly.

No single group can solve the AI challenge alone.

Conclusion

When I first started learning about data science, I thought my biggest challenge would be understanding programming languages, machine learning, and statistics. Those skills are still important, but now I think the bigger challenge is learning how to build technology that people can trust.

AI will continue improving. Governments will continue updating laws. Organizations will develop stronger governance systems, researchers will improve ethical frameworks, and technology will continue moving forward.

But I do not think those efforts will succeed independently.

The future of AI depends on governments, organizations, developers, researchers, and everyday users understanding that innovation and responsibility must develop together.

As someone who is still learning about data science, I know I have a long way to go. I will continue learning programming, machine learning, data analysis, and artificial intelligence, but I also hope I never stop asking the ethical questions that matter just as much.

Because, in the end, The AI Challenge is not simply about building smarter machines. It is about making sure that innovation, ethics, law, and society grow together.

If we can do that, I believe AI has the potential to become one of the most transformative technologies of our generation—not simply because AI is intelligent, but because we were wise enough to guide its development responsibly.
