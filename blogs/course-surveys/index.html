Is it really Anonymous?

Fritz Antonette Ardael | Jun 20, 2026 min read

The Ethical Problem Hiding in Course Surveys

Let’s be honest - most of us have filled out a course survey at some point without thinking too much about it. We click a link, answer a few questions, hit submit, and move on with our day. We usually trust that if a survey says it’s anonymous, then it is.

But what if it’s not?

Imagine receiving a survey invitation from the college you are attending. To access the survey, you’re required to log in using your organization’s username and password. Once inside, you’re told that all responses will remain anonymous. At first glance, this seems perfectly normal. However, if participants are required to log in, can the survey truly be anonymous?

This situation raises several ethical concerns that go beyond simply collecting feedback. As a data analyst, their job is not only to analyze data, but also to ensure that data is collected and used responsibly. Ethical data science requires us to think carefully about privacy, transparency, informed consent, fairness, accountability, and bias. If these principles are ignored, even a well-intentioned survey can create problems for both participants and the organization.

In this blog post, we will examine the ethical issues present in the survey design, explore additional concerns that may exist, provide recommendations to improve the process, and discuss what actions a data professional can take if management refuses to acknowledge the ethical risks.

What’s the Problem Here?

The goal of the survey is actually a good one. Executive management wants to gather student feedback to identify courses that may need updating or redesigning. Better courses mean better learning experiences, so collecting feedback makes sense. The problem isn’t the survey itself. The problem is how it’s being presented.

Participants are required to log in using their usernames and passwords before completing the survey. At the same time, they’re told that the responses are anonymous. That’s where things start to get a little uncomfortable.

Think about it from a student’s perspective. If I have to log into a system using my personal credentials, I am naturally going to wonder whether someone can connect my answers back to me. Even if the survey doesn’t ask for my name, the login system may still collect information such as:

  • Username
  • Student ID
  • Login time
  • IP address
  • Device information

The fact that this information potentially exists creates an ethical concern.

What Ethical Issues Are Apparent in the Survey Design?

Transparency

One of the most important ethical principles in data science is transparency. People should know exactly what data is being collected and how it will be used.

If students are told their responses are anonymous while the system requires authentication, there may be a gap between perception and reality. Even if management has no intention of identifying participants, the survey should clearly explain how anonymity is maintained.

Informed Consent

People should understand what they’re agreeing to before participating. If students believe their responses cannot be connected to them, but technically they can, then participants are making decisions based on incomplete information.

Privacy Concerns

Most students want to provide honest feedback. They may want to discuss issues with course content, teaching methods, or their overall experience.

However, if they think their comments could be traced back to them, they may hold back. Privacy is more than protecting data; it’s about allowing people to express themselves honestly without fear of negative consequences.

Response Bias

This concern also affects the quality of the data itself.

Students who are worried about being identified may:

  • Avoid criticism
  • Select neutral answers
  • Skip questions
  • Choose not to participate

As a result, management may receive feedback that looks positive but doesn’t accurately reflect reality. That’s called response bias, and it can seriously reduce the usefulness of survey results.

Accountability

Organizations have a responsibility to ensure their actions match their promises.

If a survey claims anonymity, management should be able to demonstrate exactly how anonymity is protected. Ethical organizations don’t simply ask for trust – they earn it through accountability.

What Other Ethical Issues Could Exist?

Beyond the obvious concerns, there are several other ethical issues worth considering.

Data Security

What happens after the data is collected? Questions that need answers include:

  • Where is the data stored?
  • Who has access to it?
  • How long is it kept?
  • Is it encrypted?

If these questions aren’t addressed, participant information could be exposed through data breaches or unauthorized access.

Function Creep

One ethical issue that often gets overlooked is function creep. This happens when data collected for one purpose is later used for another.

For example, today’s course improvement survey could become tomorrow’s instructor evaluation tool. Participants may have agreed to one use of their data but not another. That’s why organizations need clear boundaries around data usage.

Fairness

Not everyone experiences a course the same way. If certain groups are less likely to complete the survey, management may hear only a portion of the story. Decisions based on incomplete representation may unintentionally favor some groups while overlooking others. Fairness requires that all voices have an opportunity to be heard.

Sampling Bias

We’ve all seen online reviews where people are either extremely happy or extremely angry. Surveys often have the same problem. The students most motivated to respond may not represent the average student. This creates sampling bias, which can distort the conclusions drawn from the data.

Trust

Trust may not sound like a technical issue, but it is one of the most valuable assets an organization has. When people trust an organization, they are more likely to provide honest feedback and participate in future surveys. Once trust is lost, it can be difficult to rebuild.

What Recommendations Would I Make?

If I were presenting recommendations to executive management, I would focus on both ethics and data quality.

Be Honest About Anonymity

Clear communication builds trust. If responses truly cannot be linked back to individuals, explain how that works. If responses can potentially be linked, then the survey should be described as confidential rather than anonymous.

Separate Logins from Survey Responses

A better design would allow students to log in only to verify eligibility. After verification:

  1. Generate a random survey token or code.
  2. Remove identifying information.
  3. Store responses separately from login records.

This approach protects participant privacy while preventing duplicate submissions.

Improve Consent Information

Before students begin the survey, clearly explain:

  • What information is collected
  • Why it’s collected
  • Who can see it
  • How long it will be stored
  • How anonymity or confidentiality is maintained

Participants deserve this information upfront.

Strengthen Data Governance

Management should establish clear policies regarding:

  • Data access
  • Data retention
  • Data security
  • Data sharing Strong governance helps prevent ethical issues before they occur.

What If Management Rejects My Recommendations?

This is probably the toughest part of the scenario. As a data analyst, I may not have the authority to make final decisions. However, I still have professional and ethical responsibilities to raise and present these issues and propose solutions.

First, I would document all identified risks and recommendations. This creates a professional record showing that concerns were raised and addressed responsibly.

Sometimes people reject recommendations because they don’t fully understand the issue. I would continue explaining how privacy concerns and bias can impact both ethics and data quality.

If the ethical concerns are serious, I would consult internal compliance teams, privacy officers, or ethics committees.

This should always be done professionally and respectfully. At the end of the day, my responsibility isn’t just to deliver numbers. It’s to ensure those numbers were collected and interpreted responsibly.

Good data science isn’t only about finding answers. It’s also about asking whether we’re collecting data in a way that respects the people providing it.

Conclusion

At first glance, course surveys seem harmless. After all, it’s simply trying to gather feedback to improve courses. However, when we look closer, several ethical concerns emerge. Requiring participants to log in while promising anonymity creates questions about transparency, informed consent, privacy, accountability, fairness, and trust.

These concerns don’t just affect participants – they also affect the quality and reliability of the data itself. If students don’t feel comfortable providing honest feedback, management may make important decisions based on incomplete or biased information.

The good news is that these problems are preventable. Through better communication, stronger privacy protections, thoughtful survey design, and responsible data governance, organizations can collect valuable feedback while respecting the people who provide it.

As data professionals, we have a responsibility to look beyond the numbers and think outside the box. Ethical data science means remembering that behind every survey response is a real person who deserves honesty, respect, and protection. When organizations put ethics first, everyone benefits – from students and instructors to the decision-makers who rely on the data.
